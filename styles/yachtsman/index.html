A Style Profile

The Yachtsman

Dressing for a deck you may never actually set foot on, with total conviction anyway.

Nautical prep borrows its wardrobe directly from actual sailing — the double-breasted blazer with brass buttons descends from nineteenth-century British naval officer's dress, the Breton stripe from French Navy uniforms issued in Brittany in 1858 (regulation: twenty-one stripes, one for each of Napoleon's victories, or so the story goes) — and then spent the twentieth century drifting steadily away from any boat at all, adopted by yacht clubs, then East Coast prep schools, then anyone who liked the idea of a boat more than the reality of one.

The trademark features are all borrowed from deck life and kept regardless of whether there's a deck nearby: the Breton stripe, deck shoes with a non-marking sole and a lace that never quite gets tied properly, a brass-buttoned blazer worn with white trousers, a palette of navy, white, and red that reads as flag signal even indoors.

It suits people who enjoy a bit of theater in their formality — who like the idea that even a blazer can carry a story about the sea, whether or not you've personally been on one. There's an old-money cheerfulness to it, more playful than Savile Row and less worked-for than the Ivy League, borrowing gravitas from an ocean it mostly admires from the shore.

Trademark Features
  • Double-breasted navy blazers with brass buttons
  • The Breton stripe, worn as a T-shirt or a full sweater
  • Deck shoes, non-marking soles, laces rarely tied all the way
  • White trousers, worn well past any actual sailing season
  • A palette of navy, white, and red, borrowed from signal flags

The Double-Breasted Blazer

The double-breasted, brass-buttoned blazer descends directly from nineteenth-century British naval officer's dress, adopted by yacht clubs and later prep schools as a way of borrowing naval formality without any actual naval service required.

Look For

A genuine wool or wool-blend cloth (navy, not black), real brass or gold-tone buttons rather than plastic, and a proper double-breasted closure that isn't just decorative.

How To Wear It

Over white trousers with the Breton stripe shirt, or over grey flannel in cooler weather — this jacket rarely leaves the navy family of colors.

The Breton Stripe Shirt

French Navy regulation since 1858 specified exactly twenty-one stripes for the Brittany-issued sailor's shirt, one for each of Napoleon's victories according to popular legend — Coco Chanel borrowed the design for civilian wear in the 1910s and it never really left.

Look For

A genuine heavyweight cotton or cotton-wool knit, evenly spaced navy-and-white stripes, and a boat neck for the most historically accurate cut.

How To Wear It

Under the blazer or alone with white trousers — this shirt is the wardrobe's single most legible signature.

The Deck Shoe

Paul Sperry designed the original non-marking, siped rubber sole in 1935 after watching his dog's paws grip ice — the sole pattern he copied is still the reason deck shoes grip a wet boat deck, or a wet sidewalk, at all.

Look For

Genuine leather uppers, a siped (grooved) non-marking rubber sole, and rawhide laces that thread through the moccasin-style construction rather than eyelets.

How To Wear It

With bare ankles, laces never tied all the way — this shoe is meant to look slightly, deliberately undone.

The White Trouser

White trousers, worn well past any actual sailing season, borrow their formality from naval whites and cricket flannels alike — a color that only makes sense if you don't expect to do anything that would dirty it.

Look For

A genuine cotton or cotton-linen twill, a clean, flat front, and a fit that tapers without being tight.

How To Wear It

With the double-breasted blazer and deck shoes, sockless — this trouser is a warm-weather, dressed-occasion piece specifically.

The Woven Belt

A woven, often striped surcingle belt — originally a horse's saddle girth strap, adapted for menswear — carries the same borrowed-formality logic as the rest of this wardrobe: nautical or equestrian gear worn purely for its associations.

Look For

Genuine woven cotton or ribbon with leather tabs and a brass D-ring closure, and stripe colors that echo the Breton shirt or blazer buttons.

How To Wear It

With white trousers or chinos — this belt is a color accent as much as a functional piece.

Dressing For It
The same wardrobe, adjusted for what the day actually throws at it.
In Hot Weather

The blazer stays on the hanger -- just the Breton stripe and white trousers, deck shoes, bare ankles, done.

In Cold Weather

A heavier reefer coat replaces the blazer, a wool version of the striped shirt, and the deck shoes give way (reluctantly) to a proper leather boot.

In the Rain

A yellow slicker, genuinely nautical and not remotely ashamed of it, over the usual layers -- this wardrobe owns real foul-weather gear and isn't afraid to use it.

In the Snow

Effectively a foreign concept; the heaviest coat in the closet gets pressed into service along with borrowed boots from someone more prepared for it.

Dressing It Up

For the yacht club's summer ball: a proper white dinner jacket, the one true concession this wardrobe makes to genuine formality.

Beyond the Closet
A personal style was never just the clothes.
Where They'd Go

Newport, Nantucket, and the coast of Maine — always somewhere a boat can actually go.

What They'd Read

Patrick O'Brian's naval novels, sailing memoirs, and the yacht club newsletter read cover to cover.

What They'd Listen To

Jimmy Buffett, sea shanties revived without irony, and whatever's playing at the club's Friday happy hour.

How They'd Furnish a Room

Navy and white everywhere, ship's models on the mantel, and a porch built specifically for watching the water.

How They'd Spend a Saturday

Sailing, obviously, plus a standing tennis game and a genuine, competitive interest in the club's annual regatta.
