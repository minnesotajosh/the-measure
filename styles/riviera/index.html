A Style Profile

The Riviera

Tailoring that has clearly been to the beach and has no plans to apologize for it.

Resort dressing gets its blueprint from the Mediterranean coastline — Cannes, Positano, Capri — where the European wealthy spent the mid-twentieth century inventing a version of tailoring loose enough for a boat and elegant enough for dinner after. The archetype's cultural high-water mark is probably Tom Ripley, invented by Patricia Highsmith and later dressed on screen in unstructured linen suits that made ill intent look like the best-dressed decision in the room — a reminder that this style has always been a little bit about performance.

The trademark is tailoring stripped of its usual stiffness: an unstructured linen or silk-linen jacket with no real shoulder to speak of, trousers cut to be worn with bare ankles, espadrilles instead of anything requiring socks. Color leans light and warm — ecru, sky blue, coral — because the whole point is looking cool while everyone else visibly isn't.

It suits people who treat leisure as a discipline worth taking seriously — who understand that looking effortlessly good on vacation actually requires more consideration than looking good at the office, precisely because there's no dress code to hide behind. There's a genuine hedonism to it, dressed up as elegance.

Trademark Features
  • Unstructured linen suiting with essentially no shoulder padding
  • Trousers cut to be worn barefoot or with espadrilles
  • A palette of ecru, sky blue, and coral, kept light throughout
  • Camp-collar shirts worn open, never buttoned to the top
  • Sunglasses treated as a genuine accessory, not an afterthought

The Unstructured Linen Suit

The Mediterranean resort suit stripped tailoring down to almost nothing structural — Italian mills built entire lines of linen specifically loose and breathable enough to wear comfortably at 90 degrees without looking like you'd given up on dressing well.

Look For

Pure linen or a linen-silk blend, an unlined or half-lined jacket with essentially no shoulder padding, and a slightly wrinkled hand that's supposed to look that way.

How To Wear It

Jacket worn open, often without a shirt underneath buttoned all the way — this suit is not meant to be worn the way a city suit is.

The Camp-Collar Shirt

The open, notched camp collar — no top button, no way to close it even if you wanted to — became resort dressing's signature shirt precisely because it refuses the possibility of looking buttoned-up.

Look For

A soft cotton, linen, or silk-blend fabric, a notched collar that lies flat without a top button, and a boxy, slightly cropped fit meant to be worn untucked.

How To Wear It

Worn open over the shoulders of the linen suit jacket, or alone with the espadrilles and nothing else formal.

The Espadrille

The rope-soled espadrille has been Mediterranean peasant and fisherman's footwear for centuries; its adoption by the European wealthy in the mid-twentieth century was a deliberate signal that the wearer no longer needed shoes that implied labor of any kind.

Look For

A genuine jute or esparto grass rope sole, a canvas or suede upper, and enough flexibility to fold nearly flat.

How To Wear It

Barefoot, with the linen trouser breaking just above — no socks, ever, in this wardrobe.

The Swim Short

Tailored, mid-length swim shorts with a genuine drawstring waist and side pockets brought resort tailoring's sensibility even into the water, treating swimwear as a real garment rather than pure function.

Look For

Quick-dry recycled nylon or a cotton-blend twill, a fitted mid-length cut, and a mesh liner substantial enough to wear without underwear.

How To Wear It

With the camp-collar shirt worn open over it, straight from pool to lunch without changing.

The Sunglasses

On the Riviera, sunglasses function as much as a piece of jewelry as protection from glare — a genuinely well-made pair signals the same unhurried wealth as the rest of this wardrobe.

Look For

Acetate (not injected plastic) frames, genuine glass or high-grade polarized lenses, and a shape proportional to the face rather than trend-driven.

How To Wear It

Worn constantly, pushed up into the hair between uses rather than folded away — this accessory should look lived-in.

Do's and Don'ts
Every style has a right context and a wrong one, and one similar-looking garment easily mistaken for another.
Do

Buy the suit genuinely unstructured — little to no shoulder padding, a soft canvas or none at all — since the whole point is a jacket that moves like a shirt and packs like one too. Let the trousers be cut to actually go barefoot or into espadrilles; a trouser break meant for a proper dress shoe fights the rest of the outfit's logic.

Keep the palette genuinely light — ecru, sky blue, coral — and let the camp collar stay open; a buttoned-to-the-top version of this shirt undoes the whole relaxed effect it's built around.

Don't

Don't add structure back in out of habit — a shoulder pad, a fused canvas, a proper dress shoe all fight against a silhouette that's supposed to read as having just come off a boat. If it could survive a business meeting unchanged, it's not actually this style.

This is a warm-weather, resort wardrobe specifically, and it reads as underdressed and slightly odd anywhere cold or formal — the same linen suit that looks effortless on a terrace in August looks like a mistake in a heated office in November. Keep it where the climate agrees with it.

Dressing For It
The same wardrobe, adjusted for what the day actually throws at it.
In Hot Weather

Already dressed for it -- linen suit, bare ankles, espadrilles. If anything, more buttons get undone.

In Cold Weather

This wardrobe mostly doesn't own cold-weather clothes on principle; a cashmere sweater over the shoulders is about as far as it goes before retreating indoors.

In the Rain

An unwelcome interruption -- a simple linen or cotton overshirt gets thrown on, more out of protest than actual protection.

In the Snow

Effectively never happens in this wardrobe's world; if forced, borrowed clothes from someone more prepared.

Dressing It Up

The linen suit gets swapped for a proper silk-blend one for dinner, the espadrilles become loafers, and cufflinks appear for the first time all week.

Where They'd Go

The Amalfi Coast, the French Riviera, and a boat, chartered rather than owned, for the month of August specifically — ownership is considered more trouble than the month is worth.

An itinerary rarely survives contact with a good lunch; plans get rearranged around wherever the afternoon happens to be going well.

What They'd Read

Patricia Highsmith's Ripley novels, obviously, read partly for the plot and partly for the travelogue disguised inside it.

Whatever paperback thriller matches the vacation's mood exactly gets bought at the airport and abandoned, half-finished, on a shelf at the villa for the next guest.
